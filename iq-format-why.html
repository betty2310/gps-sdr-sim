A signal-processing field guide

Why store a signal as I/Q?

Because “amplitude at each time” is enough only when you keep the fast RF waveform. I/Q lets us move that waveform down to a manageable speed without losing the phase and direction information that carries data.

The short answer: I/Q is amplitude and phase, written as two coordinates. It stores the slowly changing shape of a radio signal, not every wiggle of its high-frequency carrier.

Why this matters

The carrier is fast. The information is slow.

A GPS L1 signal rides on a carrier near 1.575 GHz. If you tried to store that raw voltage waveform directly, your converter and disk would need to deal with billions of samples each second. Yet the useful signal occupies only a small band around that carrier.

A radio front end therefore removes the carrier before storage. But removing it carelessly would also erase whether the signal was ahead or behind in phase—and whether a component sat above or below the tuned frequency. I/Q downconversion keeps those distinctions.

You meet this in SDR recordings, GPS simulators, Wi-Fi and cellular receivers, radar, digital modulation, spectrum analyzers, and almost any code that performs filtering, synchronization, demodulation, or correlation on radio samples.

First, fix the premise

“Amplitude + time” is not wrong

A real ADC measures a signed voltage at each instant. If it samples fast enough, that one stream contains amplitude, frequency, and phase implicitly. The problem is not correctness; it is cost and convenience.

True

Raw real samples can store everything

Record +0.2, +0.9, +0.3, −0.7… quickly enough and the shape of the waveform reveals its phase. No separate “phase column” is required.

The catch

You must follow every carrier wiggle

A gigahertz carrier changes sign billions of times per second. Most of those stored wiggles say “the carrier is still here,” not “here is new information.”

If the receiver’s reference rotates at the same speed as the carrier, the carrier looks stationary—like jogging beside another runner. Modulation appears as the remaining slow motion of the I/Q arrow.

Common confusion: Q is not “quality,” and it is not a second antenna. It is the result of measuring the same signal against a reference shifted by one quarter-cycle.
The capability one real channel loses at baseband

I/Q preserves rotation direction

A real cosine cannot distinguish +f from −f: cos(+ωt) = cos(−ωt). But after tuning, those signs mean “above the center frequency” and “below the center frequency.” I/Q keeps them separate.

Subtract the fast spin; keep the difference

The engineering payoff

Process bandwidth, not carrier frequency

Consider an illustrative 2 MHz-wide signal centered near GPS L1. Directly sampling the carrier and complex-sampling the downconverted band solve the same capture problem at very different rates.

Direct RF

Follow a 1.575 GHz carrier

> 3.15 billion real samples/s

A simple Nyquist illustration for sampling from DC. Specialized bandpass-sampling designs can change the exact requirement.

Complex baseband

Follow a roughly 2 MHz band

about 2–4 million I/Q pairs/s

The exact rate includes filter transition room and application needs. The key scaling variable is occupied bandwidth.

Each I/Q sample has two numbers, so complex sampling is not free. But “two values at a few megasamples per second” is far cheaper than “one value at several gigasamples per second.”

What is actually inside the file?

Usually, alternating numbers

An I/Q file often stores interleaved signed integers or floating-point values. The metadata—sample rate, center frequency, numeric type, scale, and I/Q ordering—is essential because raw bytes do not describe themselves.

Amplitude changes

The point moves toward or away from the origin. Receivers use this for envelopes, gain, and amplitude modulation.

Phase changes

The point moves around the origin. PSK, carrier tracking, and coherent correlation depend on this angle.

Frequency offset

The point keeps rotating. Rotation speed and direction reveal Doppler and tuning error.

Where the mental model pays rent

What engineers do with I/Q

GNSS acquisition and tracking

Correlators compare complex samples against local PRN replicas. Carrier phase and Doppler become controlled rotations rather than billion-cycle-per-second waveforms.

Digital communications

QPSK and QAM intentionally place symbols at different I/Q coordinates. The constellation is a map of allowed amplitude-and-phase states.

Spectrum and filtering

Complex samples distinguish energy above and below the tuned center, enabling unambiguous shifts, channel selection, and analytic-signal processing.

Debugging SDR recordings

Wrong sample type, swapped I/Q, conjugation, DC offset, and I/Q imbalance create recognizable distortions. The “rotating arrow” model makes them easier to diagnose.

Knowledge check

Can you use the model?

These test reasoning, not vocabulary. Answer each one and inspect the explanation.

1. Why not store only the magnitude √(I² + Q²)?
2. An SDR is tuned to 100 MHz. One tone is at 100.2 MHz and another at 99.8 MHz. What does I/Q preserve?
3. Debug the statement: “I/Q has twice as much physical signal as a real waveform because it has two channels.”
Model answer: The front end mixes the selected RF band down to complex baseband. I/Q preserves the band’s amplitude, phase, and positive/negative frequency information, while the known carrier itself no longer needs to be sampled. The required rate therefore follows the occupied bandwidth plus filter margin, not the original carrier frequency.
Keep this sentence

The whole lesson in one thought

Real samples draw every fast wiggle. I/Q stores the slow motion of the arrow that creates those wiggles.

Both can represent the signal. I/Q is the representation that makes radio bandwidth, phase, frequency direction, modulation, and receiver algorithms manageable.
