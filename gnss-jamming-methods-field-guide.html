Why this matters

Your receiver does not report “a chirp jammer attacked me.” It reports clues: AGC moved, the FFT floor rose, acquisition found fewer PRNs, tracking C/N₀ fell, navigation bits stopped decoding, or satellites were no longer accepted into PVT.

This lesson connects those clues to the RF cause. That connection is the useful skill: waveform → spectrum → receiver stage → observable symptom.

Lab boundary Radiating GNSS interference can endanger other users and may be illegal. Keep experiments conducted over cable, inside suitable RF shielding, or under the required authorization. The simulations here are qualitative teaching models, not transmit recipes.
01

Start with a signal: a value changing over time

Before “narrowband” can mean anything, separate three properties that are often mixed together: strength, oscillation rate, and occupied bandwidth.

An antenna turns an electromagnetic field into a changing voltage. A receiver samples that voltage. The list of sample values is the signal we process.

Amplitude

How large?

Amplitude is the height of the waveform. Power grows with amplitude squared. A stronger jammer is taller, but “tall” does not tell you whether it is narrowband or wideband.

Frequency

How fast does one pattern repeat?

A steady sinusoid repeats at one frequency. It can oscillate very fast and still occupy one thin spectral line.

Bandwidth

How many frequencies are present?

Bandwidth is the width of the occupied frequency region. One tone is narrow; abrupt pulses or random sample-to-sample changes need many frequency components.

Repair this mental model

“Faster change means wider bandwidth” is only a shortcut for abrupt or random change. A perfectly steady 1.5 GHz sinusoid changes extremely fast but remains a single narrow line. Bandwidth grows when the waveform needs a range of frequencies to describe its shape: sharp edges, modulation, hopping, or unpredictable changes.

Why the receiver uses I and Q

Sampling a 1575.42 MHz carrier directly is inconvenient. The RF front end mixes a small slice around GPS L1 down near zero frequency. It stores each sample as I and Q: two coordinates of a rotating arrow. Together they preserve amplitude and phase, including whether energy lies above or below the tuned centre.

Useful reading rule

In SDR files, “0 Hz” usually means the radio’s tuned centre, not literal DC in the sky. A spectral line at +200 kHz means “200 kHz above the tuned centre.”

Sample rate sets the horizontal window

An IQ rate of Fs samples/s represents roughly one Fs-wide baseband span, from −Fs/2 to +Fs/2. For example, 4.096 Msample/s shows roughly 4.096 MHz around the tuned centre. The analog anti-alias filter must reject energy outside that span or it can fold into the recorded band.

02

Spectrum is a power map of frequency

A waveform, a spectrum, and a spectrogram are not three different signals. They are three views of the same received samples.

Waveform

x = time, y = amplitude. Best for pulses, clipping, transitions, and seeing what a single sample does.

Spectrum / PSD

x = frequency, y = power. Best for seeing lines, occupied bandwidth, spectral shape, and the noise floor.

Spectrogram

x = time, y = frequency, colour = power. Best for a jammer that moves, pulses, hops, or changes mode.

Short FFT window

Better timing, blurrier frequency

A short window tells you when a pulse or sweep arrived, but its frequency bins are wider. Use this when the event changes quickly.

Long FFT window

Sharper frequency, blurrier timing

A long window separates nearby tones, but averages motion during the window. Spectrogram design is always a time–frequency trade-off.

Narrow and wide are relative words

A 100 kHz interferer is wide compared with a 25 Hz tracking loop, but narrow compared with a receiver front end that admits 20 MHz. Always ask: bandwidth relative to what? For this lesson, compare the jammer with the useful GPS L1 C/A signal band and with the receiver’s admitted bandwidth.

Power is area, not height alone. With fixed total power, squeezing the jammer into a narrower band raises power per hertz. Spreading it wider covers more of the receiver but lowers power density unless the transmitter adds more total power.

PSD ≈ P / B

Power spectral density is power divided by bandwidth. This simple relation explains the jammer designer’s recurring trade-off: concentrate energy or cover more spectrum.

03

Why a GPS signal looks like noise on purpose

GNSS uses spread spectrum: the signal occupies much more bandwidth than the 50 bit/s navigation message needs, then the receiver collapses it again with a known PRN code.

For GPS L1 C/A, the carrier is centred at 1575.42 MHz. A satellite’s 1023-chip C/A code runs at 1.023 Mchip/s and repeats every millisecond. Ideal rectangular chips give a sinc²-like spectrum whose central lobe has first nulls at about ±1.023 MHz.

Decibels without fear

A decibel is a logarithmic ratio. It keeps huge RF power ranges readable and turns multiplication into addition. For power ratios:

+3 dBabout 2× the power
+10 dB10× the power
+20 dB100× the power
−10 dBone tenth the power

J/S compares total jammer power with desired-signal power: 0 dB means equal power, +20 dB means 100× more jammer power. That still does not predict damage without bandwidth. C/N₀, reported in dB-Hz, compares recovered carrier power with noise density and is the more familiar tracking-quality trend.

The official GPS interface specification gives a minimum received L1 C/A signal level of about −158.5 dBW (−128.5 dBm) under its stated conditions. That weakness is why a nearby terrestrial interferer can dominate the RF front end even at modest transmitted power—and why PRN correlation is essential.

The code is a key, not encryption

Each satellite uses a different public PRN sequence. Before correlation, every satellite looks noise-like and can sit below the receiver’s thermal noise. During acquisition, the receiver tries candidate PRN codes, time shifts, and Doppler frequencies. The correct combination makes 1023 tiny contributions add in phase into a peak; wrong combinations mostly cancel.

Before correlation

Signal hidden in the floor

Looking at raw IQ, a genuine C/A signal resembles noise. A higher FFT floor is useful interference evidence, but the satellite itself is not normally a bright line.

After correct correlation

Energy becomes one peak

The receiver gains processing gain by integrating the known code. Jamming wins by raising the correlator’s uncertainty, distorting the peak, or overwhelming earlier hardware.

Keep the scales separate

RF bandwidth is measured in hertz. Code delay is a horizontal shift of the PRN sequence. Doppler is a carrier-frequency offset. Acquisition searches a 2-D grid of code delay × Doppler; a spectrum alone does not show the whole search problem.

04

A jammer can break six different jobs

“The receiver lost GPS” is the final symptom. The first damaged stage tells you much more about the interference.

Stage 1

Antenna + RF

Selects the band and amplifies a very weak sky signal. A nearby jammer can dominate here.

Stage 2

AGC + ADC

Sets gain and digitizes IQ. Strong interference lowers AGC gain or clips the ADC.

Stage 3

Acquisition

Searches PRN delay and Doppler. A raised floor hides correlation peaks.

Stage 4

Tracking

DLL/PLL/FLL keep code and carrier aligned. Interference adds jitter or causes loss of lock.

Stage 5

Nav decode

Integrates symbols and checks data. Bit errors block ephemeris/time updates.

Stage 6

PVT

Uses accepted ranges. Too few or inconsistent measurements mean degraded or no fix.

Front-end evidence

Before the correlator

  • AGC command or reported gain falls
  • ADC clipping / sample histogram piles at limits
  • Total in-band power or FFT floor rises
  • Lines, sweeps, or pulses appear in a spectrogram
Receiver evidence

After correlation

  • Acquisition peaks lose margin or new PRNs are not found
  • C/N₀ trends down; code/carrier residuals grow
  • Tracking and navigation-data locks drop
  • svUsed/prUsed fall; PVT becomes invalid
One metric is never enough

High reported C/N₀ does not prove a measurement is accepted into PVT, and svUsed = 0 does not by itself prove jamming. Pair RF evidence with acquisition, tracking, measurement residuals, and PVT validity. A receiver can track a signal and still reject its pseudorange.

05

Jamming adds an unwanted signal

It does not erase the satellite waveform. It changes the mixture that reaches the receiver until one or more processing jobs can no longer separate truth from interference.

r(t) = s(t) + n(t) + j(t)

The receiver gets desired satellite signals s, ordinary noise n, and jammer j. Jammer type describes the structure of j; jammer power describes how dominant it is.

The same waveform can be harmless when weak and devastating when strong. The same power can also produce different damage depending on receiver bandwidth, filters, AGC, quantization, acquisition strategy, loop bandwidths, antenna, and whether the receiver was already tracking. There is no universal “failure at +X dB” threshold.

How to read these drawings

The carrier is deliberately slowed and the amplitude is normalized to ±A so its shape is visible. A real GPS L1 waveform cycles at 1575.42 MHz and would look like a solid blur at this scale. Real SDR hardware normally creates I/Q baseband samples and upconverts them; the plots show the equivalent real passband amplitude.

07

“Advanced” is a strategy layer, not one new shape

Cognitive, adaptive, hopping, and protocol-aware describe how a jammer chooses or controls a waveform. They should not sit beside “CW” as if all words describe the same axis.

Layer 1 · waveform

What energy looks like now: tone, multi-tone, partial-band noise, wideband noise, chirp, or pulse.

Layer 2 · targeting

What the energy is aligned to: a frequency, a GNSS band, a time window, an acquisition search, a PRN code, or a receiver state.

Layer 3 · control

How choices change: fixed, hopping, reactive, adaptive, or “cognitive.” An adaptive jammer may switch among several Layer-1 waveforms.

Counter-layer

How the receiver responds: detection/classification, notch or blanking, time-frequency excision, spatial nulling/CRPA, multi-band operation, inertial aiding, or alternate PNT.

Matched-spectrum ≠ matched-code ≠ narrowband

Matched-spectrum copies a power-versus-frequency envelope. Matched-code uses PRN structure so the target correlator responds. Narrowband only says the occupied RF frequency width is small. A PRN-coded waveform is normally spread across a GPS-like band, not narrowband.

Where this repo’s jam_noise lands

The production path in gpssim.c and both live players draws independent I/Q noise every output sample, multiplies it by the selected satellite’s current C/A chip and carrier, then adds it to the composite IQ stream.

Because independent zero-mean white noise is symmetric, multiplying each sample by ±1 C/A chips does not, by itself, create a reliable PRN correlation peak or change the white power spectrum. The current result is best taught as per-PRN-scaled, wideband noise injection, not as demonstrated target-selective matched-code jamming.

To study a genuinely structured PRN-coded interferer, first create slower symbols or a deterministic component, then spread those symbols with the chosen C/A code and verify target selectivity at the receiver correlator. That RF result is GPS-like spread spectrum, not narrowband.

// simplified from gpssim.c
ni = nextGaussianNoise(state);
nq = nextGaussianNoise(state);

// a fresh random value every sample
I = ni × codeCA × carrierI;
Q = nq × codeCA × carrierQ;

// white noise × ±1 is still white noise
compositeIQ += (I, Q);
What must be measured

A source-code comment is a design intention, not receiver-side proof. For a “target PRN” claim, compare the intended PRN with control PRNs using acquisition peak statistics, post-correlation noise, tracking residuals, and receiver acceptance. Do not infer selectivity from the transmitter waveform alone.

08

Read the receiver from outside to inside

A disciplined diagnosis follows the signal path. This prevents a PVT symptom from being mislabeled as a waveform type.

  1. Start at raw power. Did AGC fall, did samples clip, or did total in-band power rise? If not, a weak or structured attack may still exist, but broad strong jamming is less likely.
  2. Inspect spectrum and spectrogram. One line suggests CW; several lines suggest multi-tone; a fuzzy stripe suggests narrowband noise; diagonal repeats suggest chirp; vertical bars suggest pulses; a full carpet suggests wideband noise.
  3. Inspect acquisition. Count searched/found PRNs and compare peak-to- second-peak or detection statistics. Cold acquisition usually fails before an already-locked receiver loses all tracking.
  4. Inspect tracking. Trend C/N₀, DLL/PLL/FLL errors, lock time, cycle slips, and navigation-bit sync. Look for changes synchronized with the spectrogram.
  5. Inspect measurement acceptance. Check svUsed, prUsed, pseudorange residuals, and validity flags. Tracking without PVT use is evidence of a downstream consistency problem, not proof of successful navigation.
  6. Demand a control. Compare clean versus jammed, target versus untargeted PRNs, mitigation off versus on, and repeated runs. A single screenshot is a clue, not a causal result.

Compact glossary

FFT, spectrum, PSD, and spectrogram

An FFT is an algorithm that converts a finite window of samples into frequency bins. A spectrum plots magnitude or power per bin. PSD normalizes power by bandwidth. A spectrogram stacks many short spectra over time.

SNR, J/S, C/N₀, and dB-Hz

SNR compares signal and noise power in a stated bandwidth. J/S compares jammer and desired-signal power. C/N₀ compares carrier power with noise density and is expressed in dB-Hz. They are related but not interchangeable without bandwidth and measurement details.

Acquisition versus tracking

Acquisition is the broad search for a PRN’s code delay and Doppler. Tracking is the continuous fine adjustment after a signal has been found. An already-tracking receiver can often survive conditions in which cold acquisition would fail.

Jamming versus spoofing

Jamming aims mainly at denial or degradation by interference. Spoofing presents counterfeit but plausible ranging signals to manipulate measurements. Protocol-aware denial can blur the boundary, so classify the observed mechanism rather than assuming intent from one metric.
