# Synthesis Report: Anti-Jamming Techniques in Civilian GNSS Receivers (2015–2026)

---

## 1. Synthesis Overview

The 2015–2026 literature on civilian GNSS anti-jamming reveals a field that has advanced substantially from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward multi-layer, architecturally integrated systems — yet remains constrained at every stage by a persistent gap between controlled-environment performance and real-world deployment readiness. The dominant threat is empirically well-characterised: chirp-modulated personal privacy devices (PPDs) constitute the primary commercial jammer type (Mitch et al., 2011; Borio et al., 2016), while military-adjacent high-power sources produce the most operationally consequential incidents (Murrian et al., 2021; Felux et al., 2024; Osechas et al., 2022). Against this backdrop, mitigation techniques spanning six stages of the receiver chain — RF/antenna, ADC/pre-correlation, baseband transform domain, tracking loops, navigation filter, and machine learning cross-cutting — have been characterised with measurable performance metrics. The evidence base is strongest for spatial processing and baseband excision at L1 C/A frequencies; weakest for ML/DL methods in real-world conditions and for multi-frequency active mitigation in field environments. A structural tension runs throughout: techniques that most aggressively suppress interference (blind beamforming, hard ZMNL signum) also introduce artefacts (carrier-phase bias, cross-correlation distortion) that constrain their use in high-precision positioning and timing applications.

The corpus organises around six analytical themes that together span the primary research question. **T1 (Threat taxonomy)** establishes the jammer landscape as the empirical foundation. **T2 (Spatial/antenna mitigation)** is the highest-evidence theme for directional jamming rejection. **T3 (Pre-correlation excision)** addresses the ADC and pulse-domain stage critical for chirp and pulsed jammers. **T4 (Transform-domain baseband mitigation)** treats the correlator stage, where ANF and RIM frameworks compete and complement. **T5 (Tracking and navigation resilience)** covers adaptive loop bandwidth, vector tracking, INS integration, and multi-layer FDE. **T6 (ML/DL cross-cutting)** is the most active and least field-validated theme, bridging classification and mitigation with a growing simulation-to-reality gap problem.

## 3. Theme Syntheses

### T1: Threat Landscape — Civilian Jammer Taxonomy and Real-World Incidents

**Evidence strength: Strong** | 10 sources | Levels II–VII

The threat landscape for civilian GNSS is anchored by a convergent empirical core: swept-frequency (chirp) personal privacy devices (PPDs) dominate the commercial jammer market, and their signal characteristics are well-documented. The foundational measurement campaign by Mitch et al. (2011) — downgraded to Level III by the verification report because the study characterises jammer emissions rather than testing receiver responses — established that all 18 sampled devices used linear FM modulation with significant inter-device variability in sweep rate, bandwidth, and power. Crucially, effective jamming ranges exceeded manufacturer specifications by factors of 2–5. This variability is not merely an academic nuance: it directly bounds the generalisability of any fixed-parameter mitigation algorithm designed around a specific PPD profile, a point that converges with the broader detection literature surveyed by Borio et al. (2016), who show that chirp jammers dominate consumer threat while wideband Gaussian sources produce worst-case noise-floor elevation.

The macroscopic threat picture has been transformed by the LEO monitoring campaign of Murrian et al. (2021), which geolocated a Syrian jamming source to sub-kilometre accuracy and identified recurrent multi-year jamming zones in the Eastern Mediterranean, Baltic, and Black Sea — zones subsequently confirmed by large-scale ADS-B analysis for 2022 by Felux et al. (2024). The convergence across these two methodologically independent sources (direct Doppler-from-orbit vs. crowd-sourced ADS-B correlation) is notable: both identify the same geographic hotspots and both attribute operational flight impacts to these sources. The Osechas et al. (2022) instrumented A320 test flight provides the missing ground truth connecting detected jamming events to actual avionics failure modes — full GNSS outages confirmed by pre-correlation RF spectrum capture, with no C/N0 visibility to flight crew. Together, these three Level II–III field sources construct a threat environment that is demonstrably active and geographically structured, not hypothetical.

At the regulatory and infrastructure level, the Newark GBAS incident documented by Pullen & Gao (2012) remains the canonical case study of how a single \$33 PPD can repeatedly disrupt safety-critical ground infrastructure, and its lessons reappear in every subsequent survey (Ioannides et al., 2016; Morales-Ferre et al., 2020). The threat taxonomy assembled by Dovis (2015) and refined in the Proceedings of the IEEE 2016 special issue covers CW, narrowband, wideband, chirp, and pulsed classes with analytical degradation models — but the field has not produced a post-2015 systematic PPD characterisation campaign to update the Mitch et al. (2011) baseline. This is the primary empirical gap: commercial jammer designs (multi-band, higher sweep rates, USB-C form factors) have evolved, yet the open literature provides no updated device-level signal characterisation.

**Failure modes:** The threat taxonomy theme itself cannot fail, but its downstream utility is constrained by two structural gaps. First, the PPD characterisation is frozen at 2011 technology; techniques optimised for the measured chirp parameters may be suboptimal against evolved designs. Second, the transition from characterisation to quantified receiver impact depends on propagation assumptions: Morong et al. (2019) — also downgraded to Level III — shows urban attenuation substantially reduces effective range from free-space estimates, but multipath interactions with jamming signals remain unmodelled. The LEO monitoring data of Murrian et al. (2021) cannot distinguish jamming from spoofing without additional processing, creating attribution uncertainty in incidents where both may co-occur.

### T2: Spatial / Antenna-Array Mitigation (CRPA, Beamforming, Sparse Arrays)

**Evidence strength: Strong** | 10 sources | Levels III–VII

The spatial processing literature forms the most technically mature mitigation theme in the corpus, with a clear evidence hierarchy running from analytical foundations through simulation to hardware demonstrations. The Brennan (1959) and Fante & Vaccaro (2000) analytical derivations establish the theoretical ceiling for linear combining and STAP respectively — a ceiling the experimental literature consistently approaches but never reaches cleanly due to calibration imperfections. The mechanism convergence across Fernández-Prades et al. (2016), Broumandan et al. (2016), and Cuntz et al. (2016) — three independent groups reporting in the same IEEE special issue — is striking: all find that MVDR-class beamformers achieve null depths of 40 dB or greater under controlled conditions, and all identify phase/amplitude calibration as the binding constraint on real-world performance. This is not a trivial finding: it means the gap between simulation performance and deployed CRPA performance is primarily an engineering and manufacturing problem, not a fundamental signal-processing barrier.

Fernández-Prades et al. (2016) provide the strongest experimental anchor at Level III, demonstrating a GNU Radio prototype with real RF data achieving >40 dB MVDR null depth — the most-cited specific quantitative number in this theme. Critically, their open-source implementation (the GNSS-SDR framework) provides a reproducible baseline that subsequent work can build on. Cuntz et al. (2016) extend the architecture by embedding CRPA null-steering within a vector-tracking loop, demonstrating at HIL level that the combination improves performance in jamming and signal-blockage scenarios simultaneously. This joint architecture is significant because it breaks the historical separation between RF and baseband mitigation stages, suggesting that the receiver processing chain should be conceived as a coupled system rather than a cascade of independent modules.

The sparse array literature (Amin et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016) addresses a practical constraint: full CRPAs are too large and expensive for most civilian platforms. Nested and coprime sparse arrays achieve virtual apertures of order N² for N physical elements analytically, and simulation shows DoA estimation accuracy superior to uniform linear arrays at equal element count. However, this sub-theme sits entirely at Level V — simulation with no hardware prototype — and the mutual coupling characterisation problem for sparse geometries is explicitly unresolved. The DL-beamforming papers (Ramezanpour et al., 2020a, 2020b) extend into ML territory by replacing the covariance-inversion weight computation with CNN and Bi-LSTM architectures, reporting 10 dB SINR improvement over classical beamformers at 100 snapshots and up to 34 dB ISR — but remain simulation-only at Level V. These results are independently plausible but require cross-validation from non-JRC, non-Mosavi-group sources before field deployment claims can be made; no such independent validation is yet present in the corpus.

Daneshmand et al. (2016) provide a Level III result that is analytically important but often overlooked in the spatial processing literature: blind beamforming introduces carrier-phase bias that is only partially absorbed by the receiver clock state. At cm-level positioning (RTK/PPP), the differential residual after clock absorption is not negligible. This finding directly bridges T2 and the ANF/RIM bias discussion in T4, establishing that the bias artefact problem is not unique to notch filters.

**Failure modes:** The dominant failure mode for spatial mitigation is calibration: phase/amplitude calibration errors degrade null depth from the theoretical >40 dB towards 20–25 dB under real installation conditions (Fernández-Prades et al., 2016; Cuntz et al., 2016). Multipath increases sidelobe leakage, causing near-far structural interference to masquerade as directional jamming and potentially applying nulls in wrong directions. Covariance matrix estimation time sets a latency floor — for rapidly moving jammers (aircraft, vehicle-mounted), covariance may be stale by the time weights are applied. The sparse array sub-theme faces an additional failure mode: mutual coupling in non-uniform geometries distorts the assumed steering vectors, with effects not yet characterised in the open GNSS literature. For DL-based beamforming, training dataset coverage of real-world jammer diversity remains unvalidated; a novel jammer type not in the training set will receive incorrect weight updates.

### T3: Pre-Correlation Excision — Pulse Blanking, FDAF, and Robust ZMNL

**Evidence strength: Moderate–Strong** | 9 sources | Levels II–VII

The pre-correlation excision literature occupies a pivotal structural position in the receiver chain: techniques here operate before GNSS code correlation, meaning they must suppress interference without knowledge of the desired signal structure, and their imperfections propagate into every downstream stage. Three sub-streams converge in this theme: pulse blanking for chirp and pulsed interferers, FDAF for narrowband tonal interferers (particularly DME/TACAN in L5/E5a bands), and the unified robust ZMNL / RIM framework that subsumes both under a single theoretical structure.

The strongest field-validated evidence is Gao et al. (2013) at Level II — a real flight test over Frankfurt DME/TACAN high-density airspace demonstrating hybrid blanking+FDAF recovering C/N0 within 2 dB of interference-free reference for L5/E5a. This result provides external grounding for the analytical blanking models: the Bastide et al. (2004) Poisson pulse-collision model predicts up to 10 dB C/N0 degradation in European high-density airspace, the Garcia-Peña et al. (2021) corrected RTCA DO-292 formulas extend this analytically to pulsed duty cycles >20%, and Kim et al. (2025) close the loop by characterising how L5/E5a spreading code properties degrade under blanking duty cycles from 0% to 75%. The mechanism chain is coherent: the Bastide → Garcia-Peña → Kim series establishes L5 blanker theory progressively, with the Gao flight test providing empirical anchor at the most important operating point (intermediate duty cycle, realistic airspace).

Borio's (2016) pulse blanking analysis for chirp PPDs shows that swept jammers, once band-limited by the receiver front-end, become pulsed interference amenable to time-domain blanking, with 10–15 dB J/N improvement for typical PPD parameters. The physical mechanism — band-limiting transforms a continuous chirp into a duty-cycled pulse train — connects PPD mitigation directly to the DME blanking literature through the same mathematical framework. Borio & Closas's (2019) robust transform-domain framework then extends this unification: signum, Huber, and Student-t ZMNLs applied after DFT or wavelet transforms provide interference excision without requiring jammer type identification, achieving within 2 dB of interference-free acquisition sensitivity at J/N ≤ 30 dB in hardware-simulator testing. The practitioner synthesis by Borio & Closas (2017) — gray literature but analytically sound — explicitly maps signum ZMNL as reducing pulsed interference to ~3 dB effective noise elevation versus 20–30 dB unmitigated, providing the most intuitive cross-audience summary of the technique's value.

The wavelet sub-stream (Musumeci & Dovis, 2014; Mosavi et al., 2017) operates at Level V — simulation only — but establishes that wavelet packet decomposition effectively separates interference from GNSS signals across CW, narrowband chirp, and pulsed scenarios. The dual-WPT architecture of Mosavi et al. (2017) provides 15 dB additional rejection over single-stage, maintaining acquisition probability above 90% at J/N = 45 dB for CW. These results are internally consistent across the two studies but lack HIL or field validation; basis selection and threshold adaptation under varying interference power remain open engineering problems.

**Failure modes:** Threshold dependence is the structural failure mode for both blanking and excision techniques. Pulse blanking threshold must be set relative to the interference-noise floor; too low and desired signal samples are discarded (degrading tracking loop SNR), too high and interference samples are passed (negating mitigation). The optimal threshold depends on jammer power — which is unknown a priori and variable for mobile jammers. The Garcia-Peña et al. (2021) model explicitly shows model inaccuracy for duty cycles above 20%, reflecting the breakdown of stationary interference assumptions under real DME environments. For FDAF, frequency slots containing DME pulses are zeroed, which also zeros any GNSS signal energy in those slots — a distortion mechanism analogous to blanking gaps that becomes severe in dense DME environments where blanking fraction exceeds 60% (Kim et al., 2025). Beyond this duty-cycle threshold, L5/E5a correlation peak degradation reaches L1 C/A parity, eliminating the L5 signal-quality advantage. ZMNL-based RIM techniques have no known pseudorange bias, but they suppress all high-amplitude samples equally — under front-end saturation caused by extremely high J/N, the technique is moot because the AGC has already compressed the desired signal below distinguishability.

### T4: Baseband / Transform-Domain Mitigation — ANF, Wavelet, TF Analysis, and RIM

**Evidence strength: Moderate** | 10 sources | Levels III–VII

The baseband mitigation literature is theoretically the most unified of any theme in the corpus, owing to Borio & Closas's (2019) robust statistics framework that subsumes ANF, pulse blanking, and wavelet excision under a common zero-memory nonlinearity architecture. Yet this unification coexists with a concrete operational contradiction: ANF introduces pseudorange and clock bias that RIM techniques do not, creating a technique-dependent asymmetry in positioning accuracy even when both achieve similar C/N0 recovery.

The ANF loop analysis by Borio (2016) establishes the theoretical link between adaptive notch filter adaptation dynamics and frequency lock loop theory. The FLL equivalence is more than a mathematical curiosity: it provides engineers with familiar tools (loop bandwidth, Doppler bandwidth, step-size criteria) for ANF design, and it predicts the linear approximation breakdown at J/N > 40 dB — a calibration point for when to switch to RIM. The time-frequency extension by Amin et al. (2017) shows STFT-based excision outperforms ANF for chirp/FMCW interference specifically, because the STFT resolves the chirp's instantaneous frequency in the TF plane whereas the notch filter tracks a single carrier. Quadratic TFDs (Wigner-Ville, Cohen's class) offer superior TF resolution but introduce cross-term artefacts in adjacent TF bins — a failure mode that can corrupt nearby GPS signal energy if not carefully windowed.

The critical experimental contribution is Borio & Gioia (2021), which provides at Level III — real jammer IQ data captured via a specific USRP2 hardware chain processed through a custom MATLAB software receiver with real GPS/Galileo/BeiDou signals — the most direct comparison of ANF vs. RIM in both the measurement and position domains within that single experimental setup. The finding that ANF introduces 1–5 m common-mode delay bias under strong jamming while RIM techniques (signum, Huber) introduce no pseudorange bias is the most consequential quantitative result in this theme, with the explicit methodological caveat that the magnitude is measured under one front-end, one ADC quantisation, one ANF bandwidth setting, and one jammer-power profile; independent replication on a different hardware chain has not appeared in the corpus, so the 1–5 m envelope should be read as a setup-specific bound rather than a universal ANF property. It must also be noted that the ANF-vs-RIM comparison framing originates from the same JRC research group (Borio and collaborators) that authored the unifying RIM/ZMNL framework, creating a structural risk that the comparison emphasises conditions favourable to RIM; a methodologically symmetric study from a non-JRC, non-RIM-developing group would substantially strengthen the conclusion. The qualification "common-mode" is important: a bias identical across all pseudoranges is absorbed by the receiver clock state in single-point positioning and does not degrade position accuracy. However, a differential residual remains in multi-constellation processing where ANF bandwidth affects each signal type differently, and the bias is not common-mode across frequencies in dual-frequency ionospheric-correction combinations. For high-precision applications (RTK, PPP, timing), ANF is therefore disqualified as the sole mitigation technique when J/N exceeds approximately 30 dB *within the Borio & Gioia experimental envelope* — extrapolation to other receiver architectures requires explicit re-characterisation. This conclusion is corroborated — across a different measurement domain by the same author group — by Borio & Gioia (2021, GPS Solutions) [entry #43], which quantifies ANF-induced clock bias estimation delay in timing receivers; the correlated authorship means this is consistency rather than independent replication.

The multi-constellation finding from Borio & Gioia (2021) — that GPS + Galileo + BeiDou combined approximately halves positioning error compared to GPS-only under interference within their HIL setup — is a single-group JRC-sourced quantitative result. Gioia & Borio (2023) reports a directionally consistent benefit in a multi-layer cascade context but is also JRC-authored, so it does not constitute independent corroboration of the magnitude. A non-JRC anchor comes from Jafarnia-Jahromi et al. (2015) at Level III, which shows L1C/E1 CBOC provides 3–5 dB advantage over L1 C/A for CW interference — mechanically consistent with the *direction* of a multi-constellation benefit (signal diversity improves robustness) but providing no independent measurement of the *magnitude* of joint multi-constellation gain. The Ioannides et al. (2016) survey independently recommends multi-frequency, multi-constellation as a long-term resilience strategy without quantifying its standalone contribution. Synthesis position: the corpus supports the *directional* claim that multi-constellation processing reduces positioning error under jamming (positive effect likely), but the specific "halves error" magnitude is anchored on a single research group's HIL experimental chain and should be carried forward as a setup-specific reference point rather than a generalisable deployment specification until independent non-JRC field-trial replication is available.

**Failure modes:** ANF common-mode delay bias is the best-documented failure mode in this theme, as described above. The conditions under which it becomes positioning-relevant are: (a) single-frequency processing where the bias is not common-mode; (b) differential positioning where receiver-specific bias differences corrupt baselines; (c) timing applications where absolute pseudorange accuracy matters more than relative accuracy. The STFT/TFD excision sub-theme has a real-time implementation failure mode: real-time STFT at GNSS sampling rates (tens of MHz) requires FPGA or GPU acceleration; TFD cross-terms can corrupt GNSS signal energy in adjacent TF bins if not mitigated. For all transform-domain methods, the choice of transform basis is signal-type dependent — a method optimised for CW interference may perform poorly against chirp in the same domain, creating a dependency on jammer type identification that the classification theme (T6) is supposed to resolve.

### T5: Tracking-Loop and Navigation-Filter Resilience

**Evidence strength: Moderate** | 9 sources | Levels II–VII

The tracking and navigation filter resilience theme spans the widest architectural range in the corpus — from individual correlator bandwidth adaptation through vector tracking to tightly and deeply coupled GNSS/INS Kalman filter architectures. The theme's central contribution is not any single technique but the recognition that processing-chain resilience to jamming is cumulative: gains achievable at each stage are multiplicative when properly integrated, but interaction effects between stages can also produce unexpected failure cascades.

The adaptive loop bandwidth literature (Cortés et al., 2021; Cortés et al., 2023) establishes at Level V that the loop-bandwidth control algorithm (LBCA) reduces phase tracking error by 30–50% under moderate interference relative to a standard 15 Hz bandwidth PLL, while the low-complexity direct-state Kalman filter (DSKF) of Cortés et al. (2023) maintains lock 6 dB longer than classical PLL at J/N = 30 dB. These results are simulation-only and MDPI Sensors venue; the verification report flags MDPI editorial process variability. The most-cited vector-tracking quantitative anchor — Lashley & Bevly's (2010) HIL architecture study reporting a 3–5 dB J/N advantage of vector over scalar tracking, and 5–8 dB further advantage for deep-coupled MEMS IMU integration — is a 2010 simulation-and-architecture result that the present corpus does not corroborate with any post-2015 experimental measurement on either a different vector-tracking implementation or a hardware-in-the-loop platform with real jammer signals. The 3–5 dB figure should therefore be carried in this synthesis as a *legacy reference point* whose order of magnitude is theoretically defensible (Section 9 derives it from the Bayesian state-space advantage of inter-channel coupling) but whose precise magnitude under modern receiver architectures, modern jammer signal classes, and real-RF channel conditions has not been independently re-verified within the search window. Cortés et al. (2023) achieves a comparable 6 dB benefit through a mechanistically distinct route (DSKF inside a single channel rather than vector coupling across channels) which provides directional cross-corroboration of "single-digit dB" benefits from advanced tracking architectures but is not a replication of the Lashley 2010 vector-tracking measurement specifically. The mechanisms remain complementary: bandwidth adaptation reduces noise in individual channels, while vector tracking allows strong channels to assist weak ones.

Deep GNSS/INS coupling is where the evidence becomes most architecturally specific. Lashley & Bevly (2010) show — in the same 2010 HIL architecture study with the legacy-reference caveat noted above — a centralised Kalman filter outperforming a federated filter by 1–2 dB in tracking threshold, and MEMS IMU-grade inertial aiding providing 5–8 dB additional improvement in deep coupling. Lashley & Bevly (2015) then provide the critical constraint evidence at Level III with real MEMS IMU hardware: MEMS IMU deep coupling cannot bridge a 35-second continuous jamming window, with positioning error growing at 1–2 m/s drift rate. The 35-second figure is the most-cited bridging-time limit for MEMS-grade inertial in this context. This must be read alongside Cuntz et al. (2016), which demonstrates deep coupling with CRPA null-steering at HIL level — if spatial processing reduces J/N by 30–40 dB at the RF stage, the effective jamming duration seen by the inertial bridging subsystem is shortened or eliminated entirely, meaning the 35-second figure applies only to scenarios where spatial mitigation is absent or overwhelmed. Gioia & Borio (2023) demonstrate that cascaded RIM + FDE + RAIM layers maintain positioning accuracy when any single layer alone fails, with GPS + Galileo dual-constellation contributing an approximate halving of positioning error versus GPS-only within their HIL SDR experimental envelope — a Level III single-JRC-group result that should be read as directional evidence (positive multi-constellation effect under cascaded mitigation is supported) without an independent magnitude confirmation, as discussed in T4 above.

The Morales-Ferre et al. (2020) comprehensive survey provides the meta-level finding for this theme: no single mitigation technique is sufficient across the full civilian threat spectrum, and the INS-based approach is most effective for wideband/high-power jamming while spatial processing is most effective for directional single-source jamming. This differentiated recommendation has not been quantitatively unified — no single study in the corpus covers the full jammer taxonomy against the full mitigation stack.

**Failure modes:** The bridging-time limitation is the most fundamental failure mode: MEMS drift accumulates at 1–2 m/s during jamming, meaning even 10-second outages produce 10–20 m position errors before re-acquisition. Tactical-grade IMU bridging over longer durations is technically feasible but economically and physically impractical for most civilian platforms. Re-acquisition after jamming cessation is faster with deep coupling than scalar tracking (Lashley & Bevly, 2015), but the re-acquisition time under practical conditions is under-characterised. Vector tracking introduces a new failure mode: if one satellite channel provides corrupted tracking data — for example due to partial jamming or multipath — the Kalman filter update propagates the corruption to all other channels, potentially destabilising the entire tracking architecture. Multi-layer FDE (Gioia & Borio, 2023) addresses this specifically, but the cascade latency of all combined layers has not been benchmarked for real-time embedded implementation. The adaptive Kalman filter tracking of Cortés et al. (2023) has not been tested with real jammer signals, leaving its sensitivity to non-Gaussian interference uncertainty uncharacterised.

### T6: ML / DL Cross-Cutting — Classification, DL Beamforming, Simulation-to-Reality Gap

**Evidence strength: Emerging** | 8 sources | Levels V–VII

The machine learning theme is the fastest-growing area in the corpus by publication date and the weakest by evidence tier. The core tension that organises this theme is between impressive simulation performance and an unquantified real-world deployment gap — a gap that is acknowledged across the literature but not yet resolved by any field trial in the corpus.

The jammer classification line of evidence from Morales-Ferre et al. (2019) and Swinney & Woods (2021) shows SVM achieving 94.9% and CNN-based transfer learning achieving high accuracy across six jammer classes, using simulation-derived time-frequency image datasets. Both papers publish open-access Zenodo datasets, enabling reproducibility within the simulation paradigm. The classification accuracy figures — 91–95% — sound high in absolute terms but imply 5–9% misclassification rate, which means the wrong mitigation technique is selected for roughly 1 in 10 to 1 in 20 jammer encounters. In a safety-critical setting, a misclassified wideband jammer treated as CW (triggering ANF instead of blanking) could cause tracking loss where mitigation would otherwise succeed. This misclassification failure mode operates silently — the receiver applies mitigation without reporting its classification decision to the pilot or operator.

Morales-Ferre et al. (2020) survey independently reports CNN/SVM accuracy exceeding 90% for classification, corroborating the individual study numbers but drawing from the same simulation-only evidence pool. Radoš et al. (2024) provides the most recent and explicit acknowledgement: ML-based detectors outperform threshold-based methods in classification accuracy in controlled settings (>95%) but the simulation-to-reality deployment gap is "the field's primary open problem," and open-set classification — generalising to novel jammer types not in the training set — is unsolved. This is the clearest meta-statement of the ML theme's state.

The DL beamforming papers (Ramezanpour et al., 2020a — CNN; Ramezanpour et al., 2020b — Bi-LSTM+CNN) report SINR improvements of 10 dB over classical beamformers at 100 snapshots and at ISR up to 34 dB, with the Bi-LSTM claiming superior temporal modelling of chirp sweep dynamics. Both are simulation-only at Level V, and neither benchmarks inference compute cost on embedded or FPGA hardware. The practical deployment question — whether a Bi-LSTM forward pass can complete within the correlation interval of a GNSS receiver at 50 Hz tracking — is unaddressed. The Mosavi & Shafiee (2016) early ML paper reports a Sigma-Pi neural network improving residual CW suppression by ~5 dB beyond cascade ANF, also simulation-only, and represents the earliest ML entry in this theme.

The mechanism by which DL might outperform classical techniques is plausible: neural networks can learn non-linear interference-signal boundaries that fixed ZMNL or linear beamformers cannot represent. But this advantage is only manifest when the inference-time jammer is drawn from the same distribution as the training set. The distribution shift problem — the central challenge of deploying ML in operational environments — is present in every ML paper in this theme and resolved in none.

**Failure modes:** Open-set classification failure is the dominant failure mode: a jammer type not represented in training — whether a novel commercial device, a government asset, or a modified PPD — will be misclassified with no reliable detection of the misclassification. Training-dataset representativeness is the underlying dependency: all current classification work uses either simulated or the specific limited real-device samples from Mitch et al. (2011), which is frozen at 2011 jammer technology. Inference latency on embedded hardware is uncharacterised — this is a methodological gap, not a known failure, but constitutes a deployment barrier. The simulation-to-reality domain gap is compounded by the dynamic channel: real-world jammer signals arrive via multipath, pass through real AGC compression, and interact with front-end nonlinearities in ways that simulation datasets do not currently capture. Transfer learning from ImageNet (Swinney & Woods, 2021) reduces training data requirements but introduces a domain mismatch — RF interference patterns differ fundamentally from natural image statistics — that requires domain adaptation work not yet present in the corpus.

## 6. Failure-Mode Synthesis (Layer-3 Deliverable)

### 6.0 Evidence-Provenance Classification

To avoid conflating qualitatively different failure-mode evidence — a methodological concern flagged during the synthesis quality checkpoint — each failure mode below is tagged with one of three provenance categories:

- **[Observed-incident]**: The failure mode has been documented in real-world deployment with field measurement or operational incident data. Existence at deployment scale is empirically established; the open question is its frequency and quantitative magnitude across other deployment contexts.
- **[HIL-demonstrated]**: The failure mode has been reproduced in hardware-in-the-loop testing with real or realistic signals on real receiver hardware. Existence is experimentally established under controlled conditions; the open question is the extent to which the HIL setup generalises to field deployment.
- **[Theory-predicted]**: The failure mode follows from analytical reasoning, simulation, or extrapolation from related domains, but has not been experimentally confirmed in HIL or field environments within this corpus. Existence is plausible and mechanistically derivable; the open question is whether the predicted failure manifests in practice and at what magnitude.

These tags should not be read as a ranking — a theory-predicted failure mode (such as open-set ML misclassification) may pose greater operational risk than an observed-incident mode with known mitigations — but they are essential for distinguishing what the corpus *demonstrates* from what it *predicts*, particularly when this synthesis feeds downstream recommendations.

### 6.1 Front-End Saturation: The Upstream Wall — [Observed-incident + Theory-predicted]

All baseband mitigation techniques share a common upstream failure mode that is often understated in the individual-paper literature: AGC nonlinearity and ADC clipping under extreme J/N. When jammer power at the antenna exceeds the AGC dynamic range — typically around 40–50 dB above thermal noise for civilian front-ends (Kaplan & Hegarty, 2017) — the AGC compresses both the jammer and the desired GPS signal, and the ADC clips both. Beyond this saturation point, the signal amplitude information required by baseband mitigation is destroyed before it reaches the correlator. Borio & Gioia (2021) note explicitly that high J/N jamming can saturate the front-end "before RIM is effective," and Borio & Gioia (2021, GPS Solutions) confirm that even multi-constellation timing receivers require additional oscillator holdover at very high J/N. This establishes a hard physical ceiling: baseband mitigation is bounded above by front-end headroom, and any study reporting performance at J/N = 60 dB (Aghadadashfam et al., 2021) should be interpreted cautiously unless front-end saturation behaviour is explicitly modelled. Front-end saturation is the one failure mode that no amount of algorithmic sophistication can overcome without hardware changes (higher-dynamic-range ADC, pre-amplifier design).

### 6.2 Calibration and Phase Coherence: The CRPA Constraint — [HIL-demonstrated]

For spatial processing, the binding real-world constraint is phase and amplitude calibration. The theoretical null depth of MVDR beamformers exceeds 40 dB (Fernández-Prades et al., 2016), but calibration errors in real antenna arrays — arising from mutual coupling, cable length mismatches, component tolerances, thermal drift, and installation geometry deviations — reduce achievable null depth to 20–25 dB in practice. Cuntz et al. (2016) identify array calibration as the primary deployment barrier. Fante & Vaccaro (2000) show that STAP performance degrades with covariance estimation accuracy. Daneshmand et al. (2016) add a subtler point: even when null depth is maintained, blind beamforming introduces carrier-phase bias that propagates to positioning accuracy for RTK/PPP applications. The sparse array sub-theme (Amin et al., 2016) adds mutual coupling characterisation as an unresolved open problem specific to non-uniform geometries. Calibration failure is not a binary event: it produces a gradual degradation in interference rejection that may remain invisible to the receiver's internal diagnostics while steadily eroding position accuracy. The combination of phase bias (Daneshmand et al., 2016) and calibration-induced sidelobe leakage means spatial mitigation deployment in high-precision applications requires continuous array monitoring — a capability not standardly present in commercial GNSS receivers.

### 6.3 Threshold Dependency: Blanking Duty Cycle and Notch Bandwidth — [HIL-demonstrated + Theory-predicted]

Both pulse blanking and ANF require parameter settings that must balance interference rejection against desired signal preservation. For pulse blanking, the threshold determines the duty cycle: too low and signal samples are discarded, too high and interference passes. The Garcia-Peña et al. (2021) model explicitly captures this trade-off, and Kim et al. (2025) quantify the downstream consequence for L5/E5a correlation properties — above 60% duty cycle blanking, correlation peak degradation reaches L1 C/A parity. In airborne environments over European DME high-density corridors, duty cycles above 30% are reported (Bastide et al., 2004; Gao et al., 2013), meaning the L5 quality advantage erodes substantially in exactly the environments where it is most needed for aviation precision approach. For ANF, the step-size and bandwidth parameters control convergence rate and tracking accuracy; Borio (2016) shows that the linear approximation for step-size design breaks down at J/N > 40 dB. In operational scenarios, jammer power is unknown and time-varying — a PPD in a vehicle passing a receiver produces a sweep in J/N over seconds — requiring adaptive threshold management that none of the corpus papers demonstrates in real-time embedded implementation.

### 6.4 Untested and Simulation-Only Failure Modes: ML and DL Claims — [Theory-predicted]

The ML theme is the most extreme case of simulation-only validation in the corpus. Every classification and DL beamforming paper reviewed sits at Level V (simulation) with no field-validated or even HIL-validated performance. The failure modes that cannot be detected from simulation include: (a) AGC compression and ADC quantisation noise on real jammer IQ, which alter feature statistics that the classifier was trained on; (b) multipath-induced signal structure at the antenna that produces time-varying amplitude and phase modulation absent in channel-free simulation; (c) front-end hardware variability across receiver models — the same jammer produces different IF waveforms in different commercial receivers. Transfer learning from ImageNet features (Swinney & Woods, 2021) introduces an additional mismatch: the statistical properties of RF interference spectrograms are fundamentally different from natural image statistics, and feature reuse validity requires empirical demonstration. None of these failure modes can be characterised without real hardware integration, which represents the most urgent experimental gap in the field.

### 6.5 INS Bridging-Time Limits and Drift Accumulation — [HIL-demonstrated]

Lashley & Bevly (2015) establish that MEMS-grade IMUs accumulate position error at approximately 1–2 m/s during GNSS outages, bounding the utility of deep coupling to jamming durations below approximately 10–35 seconds depending on application accuracy requirements. The 35-second figure is an outer bound for position-error accumulation to the order of tens of metres. This limit is intrinsic to MEMS sensor physics: gyro drift and accelerometer bias grow with time, producing position uncertainty that compounds quadratically under double integration. The mitigation architecture context matters: when CRPA null-steering is co-present, effective jamming duration is shortened and INS bridging may never be activated for moderate J/N. For high-power wideband jamming that overwhelms even spatial mitigation (scenarios reported by Murrian et al. (2021) and Osechas et al. (2022) for Eastern Mediterranean sources), the INS bridges an outage of unknown duration. Re-acquisition after cessation is faster with deep coupling than scalar tracking (Lashley & Bevly, 2015), but re-acquisition time under diverse post-jamming signal conditions is under-characterised across the corpus — particularly for multi-constellation receivers where re-acquisition of multiple constellations simultaneously may interact with tracking loop dynamics.

### 6.6 Open-Set Classification Failure: The Novel Jammer Problem — [Theory-predicted]

All ML classification systems in the corpus are trained on closed-set assumptions: a fixed number of jammer classes corresponding to the types in the training dataset. Radoš et al. (2024) explicitly identifies open-set classification — generalising to jammer types not in the training set — as unsolved. In the real threat environment, this is not a hypothetical concern: commercial jammer technology evolves, government assets may be employed in civilian contexts, and improvised devices built from software-defined radio platforms can combine characteristics of multiple jammer classes in ways not anticipated by training datasets. An open-set failure produces a classification output (some known class label) with no mechanism for flagging that the input is out-of-distribution. The downstream consequence is that the mitigation stage receives a confident but incorrect technique selection — for example, applying ANF (appropriate for CW) to a wideband source, providing no benefit while consuming processing resources. The structural fix — open-set recognition with a rejection class and confidence scoring — is established in the general ML literature but not yet demonstrated in the GNSS interference context.

### 6.7 ANF Common-Mode Delay Bias for High-Precision PNT — [HIL-demonstrated, single-group]

The ANF bias failure mode deserves separate treatment because it is well-quantified (1–5 m under strong jamming, Borio & Gioia, 2021) but operates silently in standard receiver designs. The common-mode nature means single-frequency single-constellation positioning receivers absorb most of the bias in the clock state, reporting apparently reasonable position accuracy while carrying a systematic offset. The failure mode activates specifically when: (a) dual-frequency ionospheric correction is applied, since the bias magnitude is frequency-dependent; (b) differential processing is used, since receiver-specific bias differences corrupt baselines; (c) timing infrastructure uses the pseudorange absolute value for clock synchronisation. The railway, maritime, and aviation precision approach use cases where ANF is an appealing low-cost mitigation are precisely the use cases most likely to be affected by this silent bias. The resolution from Borio & Gioia (2021) is clear: use RIM (signum or Huber ZMNL) rather than ANF for any application requiring pseudorange accuracy better than a few metres under moderate-to-strong jamming.

3. **Sparse array GNSS anti-jamming hardware demonstration** | Type: *Empirical* | Implication: The theoretical O(N²) virtual aperture advantage of nested and coprime sparse arrays is analytically established, but mutual coupling in non-uniform geometries and real-hardware DoA estimation accuracy under in-band GNSS signals remain uncharacterised. ## 9. Theoretical Integration

The anti-jamming literature reviewed here is grounded in three intersecting theoretical traditions that provide the deepest explanatory coherence for empirical results.

The first is **robust statistics** (Huber's M-estimator theory, signum / Lp-norm optimisation). The RIM/ZMNL framework of Borio & Closas (2019) is mathematically a special case of robust estimation: the signum nonlinearity is the maximum-likelihood estimator under a Laplacian noise model, the Huber function transitions between Gaussian (small residuals) and Laplacian (large residuals) regimes, and the Student-t soft function provides heavier-tail robustness. This framing explains why RIM introduces no bias while ANF does: ANF is a recursive linear filter operating on the full signal, so its steady-state response includes a carrier-phase shift; ZMNLs operate memorylessly on each sample, preserving the carrier-phase structure of the surviving samples. Borio's (2016) ANF loop analysis deepens this by showing the FLL-equivalent dynamics — the ANF adaptation is governed by the same loop-stability criteria as a frequency tracking loop, with identical bandwidth-vs-noise trade-offs. This theoretical unification predicts the linear-model breakdown at high J/N as a form of loop stress beyond the small-signal linearisation range.

The second tradition is **array processing** (Brennan, 1959; Fante & Vaccaro, 2000; Van Trees' DMOA framework). The MVDR beamformer, STAP, and eigenspace null-steering all derive from the same minimum-variance distortionless response criterion: minimise output power subject to unity gain in the desired direction. This criterion is optimal under Gaussian interference statistics, and its well-known sensitivity to steering vector errors (the performance degradation under phase calibration error documented by Fernández-Prades et al., 2016) follows directly from the matrix inversion at its core. The theoretical connection between STAP and tracking loops (Cuntz et al., 2016 combine them) is expressed through the Wiener-Hopf equations: both are implementations of the linear minimum mean-square error estimator in different signal domains.

The third tradition is **Bayesian recursive estimation** — specifically the Kalman filter formulations that underpin vector tracking (Lashley & Bevly, 2010) and tightly/deeply coupled GNSS/INS (Groves, 2013). The performance advantage of vector tracking over scalar tracking is a direct consequence of exploiting inter-channel correlations in the state space: the centralised filter uses satellite geometry to share measurement information across channels, producing a jointly optimal estimate that no channel-by-channel scalar filter can match. The 3–5 dB JSR improvement over scalar tracking from Lashley & Bevly (2010) is the canonical empirical reference for this theoretical advantage — though, as noted in Section 3.5, this specific magnitude is a 2010 HIL-architecture result not independently re-measured in the post-2015 corpus; modern implementations of vector tracking on contemporary FPGA receivers against contemporary jammer classes may produce different numeric values while preserving the qualitative inter-channel-coupling benefit predicted by the Bayesian framework. The DSKF of Cortés et al. (2023) is the direct-state (non-discriminator) version of the same Bayesian framework applied within a single tracking channel, replacing the phase discriminator with an optimal measurement model — a step that improves noise performance by eliminating the discriminator's linearisation error, and which provides a partial modern echo of the single-digit dB family of benefits.

## 10. Synthesis Limitations

This synthesis cannot claim to be a quantitative meta-analysis. The corpus exhibits pervasive metric incommensurability: studies report C/N0 recovery (dB), J/N tolerance (dB), acquisition probability (%), tracking error (rad), position error (m), clock bias (ns), classification accuracy (%), or SINR (dB), often under incomparable jammer types, input signal levels, and receiver front-end configurations. No cross-metric ranking is possible or attempted; the evidence map in Section 5 avoids rank ordering by design.

JRC institutional concentration (approximately 16% of the corpus across entries #9, #24, #25, #27, #30, #31, #32, #42, #43) creates a residual corroboration risk that the synthesis has attempted to flag wherever JRC-sourced quantitative claims lack independent replication. The multi-layer and multi-constellation benefits are the most concentration-affected findings. Following Devil's Advocate Checkpoint 2 review, two specific demotions have been applied: (a) the "GPS + Galileo + BeiDou approximately halves positioning error" quantitative claim from Borio & Gioia (2021) is now carried as a *directional* finding (positive multi-constellation effect supported) with the specific magnitude flagged as a single-research-group setup-specific reference point rather than a confirmed deployment specification; and (b) the ANF-vs-RIM bias comparison framing is now explicitly attributed to its origin within the RIM-developing research group, with the 1–5 m bias envelope scoped to the specific USRP2 + MATLAB experimental setup of Borio & Gioia (2021) rather than asserted as a universal ANF property. A third revision tightens the Lashley & Bevly (2010) 3–5 dB vector-tracking advantage and 5–8 dB deep-coupling figures: these are 2010 HIL-architecture results that have not been independently re-measured in the post-2015 corpus on modern receiver hardware against modern jammer classes, and are accordingly carried as legacy reference points whose order of magnitude is theoretically defensible but whose precise values warrant re-verification in subsequent work. A fourth methodological revision distinguishes failure modes in Section 6 by evidence provenance (observed-incident, HIL-demonstrated, theory-predicted) to prevent the implicit conflation of demonstrated and predicted failure modes.

The verification report's tier downgrades for Mitch et al. (2011) and Morong et al. (2019) have been applied throughout; 86% of the corpus was not individually spot-checked beyond structural audit, carrying residual author-substitution risk that the Felux et al. (2024) correction illustrates concretely.

The ML/DL theme evidence is simulation-dominated with no field-validated entries, which means the synthesis of that theme characterises the state of the research programme rather than the state of deployed technique performance. The corpus is English-only; active German-language DLR work and Chinese BeiDou anti-jamming research appear only through English-language publications, potentially underrepresenting both. Finally, the literature search has a May 2026 cutoff; given the pace of publication in ML/DL applied to GNSS, material advances may have appeared in the months preceding this synthesis that are not reflected here.
